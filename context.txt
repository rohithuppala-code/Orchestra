## 2. What Is ORCHESTRA?

ORCHESTRA is a research framework for studying how organizational structures emerge and evolve within societies of autonomous AI agents without explicitly assigning predefined roles such as leader, worker, planner, or reviewer.

The agents initially operate with minimal role specification. They interact, communicate, make decisions, and coordinate to complete tasks in controlled environments.

The research investigates whether repeated interactions naturally lead to the emergence of:

- Behavioral roles and specialization.
- Leadership or influence patterns.
- Coordination strategies.
- Communication and interaction structures.
- Organizational stability and evolution.
- Adaptation and recovery after disruptions.

These are research questions, not guaranteed outcomes. ORCHESTRA must measure actual agent behavior rather than assume that organizational roles or effective leadership will emerge.

The objective is not merely to build another multi-agent application. It is to understand how agent societies organize themselves, how their organization changes over time, and how organizational patterns relate to collective outcomes.

## 3. Research Problem

Many multi-agent systems use predefined roles, fixed workflows, explicit hierarchies, or manually designed coordination structures.

ORCHESTRA investigates:

**How do organizational structures emerge and evolve in autonomous AI-agent societies without explicitly assigned fixed roles, and how do these structures influence collective performance, stability, adaptation, and resilience?**

The framework should examine organizational behavior under different experimental conditions, including:

- Number of agents.
- Task complexity and task coupling.
- Communication protocols and constraints.
- Agent capabilities.
- Information and resource availability.
- Changes in tasks or environmental conditions.
- Agent removal and communication disruptions.

The research should distinguish correlation from causation. For example, an agent that sends many messages is not automatically a leader, and a high-performing group does not automatically possess an effective organizational structure.

## 4. Research Objectives

### Objective 1: Emergent Roles and Specialization

Investigate whether agents develop distinct behavioral patterns without being explicitly assigned roles.

Possible evidence includes task contributions, tool usage, information aggregation, communication patterns, and recurring behavior across rounds and repeated runs.

### Objective 2: Organizational Structure

Analyze communication and interaction graphs to understand how agents connect and coordinate.

Potential measurements include communication density, centrality, connectivity, network structure, topology changes, and structural stability.

### Objective 3: Organization and Collective Performance

Investigate how observed organizational patterns relate to task success, partial correctness, communication cost, token consumption, execution time, and other relevant performance measures.

### Objective 4: Organizational Evolution

Study how behavioral patterns and communication structures change over time, across tasks, and across experimental conditions.

Longitudinal observations are important. A single final output is insufficient to establish organizational evolution.

### Objective 5: Adaptation and Resilience

Study how an organization responds to controlled perturbations, such as removing an agent, restricting communication, redistributing tasks, changing agent capabilities, or modifying available resources.

Potential measurements include performance degradation, structural reorganization, adaptation, and recovery.

### Objective 6: Human-in-the-Loop Control

ORCHESTRA includes a Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) component to monitor organizational evolution and prevent excessively aggressive or unintended structural changes.

Human oversight may constrain or guide organizational changes when they exceed defined experimental boundaries, while preserving agent autonomy and avoiding predefined organizational roles.

The implementation must eventually define measurable intervention conditions and record when and why human oversight is applied. HITL behavior must not be claimed as implemented until it actually exists and has been tested.

### Objective 7: Reproducible Research

Record agent interactions, system states, task execution, communication, experimental configurations, and outcomes so that organizational behavior can be analyzed and experiments can be reproduced.

## 7. Scientific Limitations and Risks

### Coordination Hints

Some task descriptions may contain algorithmic guidance or suggested topologies. Such hints could influence the structures that agents develop.

Inspect the prompts and metadata. Where appropriate, compare conditions with and without hints while preserving valid task execution and evaluation.

### Role Emergence

A role-free prompt does not prove that roles have emerged independently. Behavior may be shaped by agent IDs, private inputs, task hints, model characteristics, or other asymmetries.

Roles must be operationally defined and supported by observable, repeatable evidence.

### Leadership and Influence

Communication centrality alone does not prove leadership or causal influence. Distinguish network position from evidence that an agent affected another agent's decisions or the final outcome.

### Stability and Evolution

Repeated observations and round-level logs are needed to assess stability and evolution. A single run cannot establish robust organizational behavior.

### Perturbation Support

The existing benchmark may not support dynamic agent removal, message loss, message delay, or persistent memory across tasks. Verify the actual implementation. Any missing capability must be added deliberately and documented rather than assumed to exist.

### Model Dependence

Organizational behavior can depend on the selected LLM. Record the model identifier, version or tag, quantization when available, inference settings, and relevant runtime details.

Results from different models must not be treated as directly interchangeable without suitable controls or analysis.

## 8. Experimental Direction

The immediate goal is to validate the implementation pipeline, not to run a massive experiment matrix.

### Initial Pilot

- Select one suitable, small SILO-BENCH task.
- Start with two agents.
- Start with one communication protocol.
- Connect the agents to a local Ollama model.
- Execute the task through the actual benchmark framework.
- Verify agent inputs, communication, execution, evaluation, and logs.
- Confirm that the experiment can be repeated.

### Next Stage

After the two-agent pilot works, validate the system with five agents.

Check execution time, resource consumption, communication, correctness, and repeatability before expanding the experimental scope.

### Later Research Experiments

Once the baseline is reliable:

- Select representative tasks.
- Compare communication protocols.
- Conduct repeated runs.
- Analyze organizational and behavioral metrics.
- Investigate structural and performance changes under controlled perturbations.
- Introduce additional agent scales only when justified by the research questions and available resources.

Do not commit prematurely to hundreds or thousands of runs. Finalize the experiment matrix after pilot results, runtime, costs, and statistical requirements are understood.

### 1. Primary Goal

**ORCHESTRA's primary objective is to produce a rigorous, novel, experimentally validated research paper targeting publication in a Q1-ranked journal.** Building a working software system is necessary, but it is not the final goal. The system must be developed specifically to generate credible scientific evidence that can support a strong journal submission.

Every architectural decision, implementation task, experiment, and analysis must serve this objective. Do not treat this as a normal student project, a basic multi-agent demonstration, or a software engineering exercise. Treat it as a serious research program whose final deliverable is a defensible scientific contribution.

Q1 publication is the target we must work toward as a team. No one can guarantee a journal's acceptance decision, but we must maximize the probability of success through novelty, methodological rigor, high-quality experiments, reliable evidence, appropriate statistical analysis, clear scientific contributions, and a carefully selected target journal.

### 2. Research Results Are Mandatory

The project must produce actual experimental results, not assumed outcomes, illustrative numbers, fabricated data, or claims based only on a successful software run.

The final research must answer clearly defined research questions using evidence generated by executing experiments. Results may support or reject our hypotheses; both outcomes are scientifically valid. Never alter experimental design or selectively report findings simply to make ORCHESTRA appear successful.

The implementation must enable us to:

- Execute SILO-BENCH tasks through a reliable, configurable experimental pipeline.
- Observe and record agent behavior, communications, task execution, interactions, organizational changes, and outcomes.
- Investigate whether behavioral specialization, leadership-like influence, coordination structures, or other organizational patterns actually emerge without explicitly assigning those roles.
- Quantify organizational properties using operationally defined, validated metrics.
- Investigate relationships between organizational structure and collective performance without confusing correlation with causation.
- Evaluate how organizations respond to controlled disruptions, changing conditions, and interventions.
- Compare experimental conditions against appropriate baselines and controls.
- Repeat experiments and quantify variability and uncertainty.
- Preserve raw data, failed runs, experiment configurations, analysis scripts, and derived results.

### 3. Scientific Novelty Must Be Demonstrated

Do not assume that ORCHESTRA is novel merely because it uses autonomous agents, role-free prompts, multiple communication protocols, or a benchmark that already exists.

Before finalizing the research contribution, conduct a focused and systematic literature review of relevant work on multi-agent systems, emergent specialization, organizational dynamics, collective intelligence, agent communication networks, resilience, adaptation, and human oversight.

Identify the precise research gap, explain why existing approaches do not adequately answer it, and establish how ORCHESTRA addresses that gap. Select the research questions, hypotheses, baselines, metrics, and experiments to directly test the proposed contribution.

Potential contributions include longitudinal measurement of organizational evolution, quantitative relationships between organizational structure and performance, controlled studies of organizational resilience, reproducible behavioral instrumentation, and human-in-the-loop oversight of organizational change. These are candidate contributions until the literature review and experimental evidence establish their significance.

If the initial concept lacks sufficient novelty for a competitive Q1 submission, identify the weakness early and recommend the smallest scientifically justified changes needed to strengthen the contribution. Do not hide novelty gaps or proceed under the false assumption that a working implementation guarantees publishability.

### 4. Publication-Quality Experimental Design

Experiments must be designed to answer research questions, not merely to demonstrate that agents can communicate.

The experimental protocol must define, where relevant:

- Independent variables, dependent variables, controls, and confounding factors.
- Research questions and testable hypotheses.
- Baselines and comparison conditions.
- Task selection, agent scales, communication protocols, model configurations, and experimental repetitions.
- Conditions with and without coordination hints when needed to distinguish genuine behavioral emergence from task-provided guidance.
- Controlled perturbations and appropriate recovery measurements.
- Failure handling, exclusion criteria, and treatment of incomplete runs.
- Statistical methods, uncertainty estimates, effect sizes, and appropriate significance testing.
- Limitations, threats to validity, and the scope of conclusions supported by the evidence.

Determine the final experimental matrix only after the literature review, pilot experiments, resource assessment, and statistical planning. Begin with small runs to validate the pipeline, then scale the experiments according to the research design. Initial two-agent or five-agent tests are implementation validation, not sufficient evidence for a Q1 paper.

### 5. Reproducibility and Research Integrity

Every experiment must be traceable and reproducible to the extent technically possible. Record the model and version, inference parameters, task configuration, agent count, communication protocol, random seeds where supported, software versions, experiment identifiers, timestamps, errors, raw logs, and analysis configuration.

Separate raw observations from derived metrics and interpretations. Validate instrumentation and metrics before relying on them. Preserve unsuccessful runs and document deviations from the planned protocol.

Never fabricate results, silently change benchmark evaluation, invent citations, overstate statistical significance, label agents as leaders without an operational definition and supporting evidence, or claim that ORCHESTRA outperforms a baseline without an appropriate comparison.

If a measurement cannot be validated, explicitly document the limitation and improve or replace it before using it to support a major scientific claim.

### 6. Human-in-the-Loop Research Requirement

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) oversight is part of the ORCHESTRA research concept. It must be treated as a research component, not merely a descriptive feature.

Define what the oversight mechanism observes, which organizational changes or boundary violations can trigger intervention, what actions a human can authorize or restrict, and how autonomy and intervention are measured.

Where HITL is claimed as a contribution, design suitable experiments to compare relevant oversight conditions and measure its effects on organizational behavior, safety constraints, task performance, adaptation, and recovery. Do not claim that HITL improves outcomes unless the results demonstrate that conclusion.
